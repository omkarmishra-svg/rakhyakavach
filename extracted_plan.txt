
Frontend Design System
“Sentry Floor” — Dashboard Design for the Factory Safety AI Prototype

1. Design Brief
Subject: a real-time monitoring dashboard for factory safety officers watching live PPE compliance and fire/smoke detection across a plant.
Audience: in the demo, hackathon judges; in reality, EHS (Environmental Health & Safety) managers and floor supervisors — people who glance at this screen for seconds at a time, often from across a room, and need to know instantly whether something needs their attention.
Primary job of the screen: surface the one thing that matters (an active hazard) faster than a human scanning a bank of raw CCTV feeds could — and do it in a way that reads as credible industrial software, not a generic startup SaaS demo.
Why this matters for judging: in a hackathon, the frontend is the only part most judges actually experience. A dashboard that looks like a templated admin-panel kit undersells a genuinely good detection pipeline. A dashboard that looks like it belongs in a real plant control room signals the team understood the domain, not just the model.

2. Where the Design Comes From
Instead of borrowing a generic SaaS look, this design is grounded in the actual visual language of industrial safety:
ANSI Z535 safety colors — the same red/amber/green coding used on real hazard signage, gauges, and alarm panels — used here functionally only (to mean danger/warning/safe), never decoratively.
Control-room instrumentation — flat rectangular panels, hairline borders, near-zero border-radius. Real industrial hardware doesn’t have rounded plastic-app corners or soft drop shadows; this dashboard doesn’t either.
The live feed as the literal hero — not a marketing headline or a big stat. The first thing anyone sees is the actual detection happening, because that live, provable moment is the product’s real value.
This deliberately avoids the current default “AI-generated dashboard” look: no warm cream-and-terracotta palette, no near-black-with-single-neon-accent, no identical rounded SaaS cards with soft grey shadows, no all-caps eyebrow labels or decorative middle-dot meta strings.

4. The Screen

Sentry Floor dashboard mockup
What’s on screen and why: - Top status bar — plant name, live compliance %, active violation count, hazard count, cameras online. This is what a supervisor checks in the first two seconds. - Hero live feed — the actual camera with bounding boxes drawn live: green for compliant workers, amber for PPE violations, red for fire/smoke — directly reusing the same three status colors, so the visual language is consistent from the top bar down to individual boxes. - Zone status rail — every monitored zone at a glance, colored dot + one-line detail, so a viewer can tell plant-wide health without watching every camera. - Violations trend (bottom of rail) — a 12-hour bar trend, colored by severity. This is what turns the dashboard from “an alert box” into “an insights tool,” directly answering the PS’s ask for actionable insights. - Incident log ticker — timestamped, zoned, confidence-scored, with an action state (alert sent / logged) — this is the audit trail that makes the system usable as evidence, not just a notification.

5. Screen Flow

Screen flow diagram
Live Dashboard is the default/home screen (the one shown above) — everything else is one click deeper.
Incident Detail opens from clicking any ticker row or bounding box: full snapshot, confidence, zone history for that violation type.
Zone Deep-Dive opens from clicking a zone in the rail: that camera’s feed plus its own violation history.
Compliance Analytics is a dedicated view for trend charts, per-zone breakdowns, and exporting a report (ties back to the “audit-ready evidence” business angle from your plan document).
Alert Actions (acknowledge/escalate/resolve) live inside Incident Detail — this is what proves the “actionable” part of the PS, not just detection.
For a hackathon demo, build the Live Dashboard first — it’s the screen judges will actually see and remember. Incident Detail and Analytics are worth having as a second screen if time allows, but a polished single screen beats four half-built ones.

6. Principles to Hold Onto While Building
Color is a signal, not a theme. Keep 95% of the UI neutral graphite. The instant something is wrong, it should visually punch through — that only works if color is scarce everywhere else.
The live feed is the hero, always. Don’t bury it behind stat cards or a marketing-style header. It’s the proof the system works.
Flat over rounded. No soft drop shadows, no heavy border-radius — panels read as instruments, not app cards.
Numbers get to be big. Compliance %, alert counts — size them like a real gauge readout.
One accent moment, not scattered motion. If you add any animation, make it the moment a new alert lands in the ticker (a brief highlight flash) — not hover effects on every element.
Say what happened, plainly. “NO HELMET · Paint Booth · confidence 0.91” beats a vague “Alert triggered” — the interface should read like an instrument, stating facts, not narrating them.
